  1. 📋 AED is NOT available in this scenario.
  2. Verified scene safety; identified which resource being used (ARC or AHA) (2 pts)
  3. Opened first aid kit; put on PPE (2 pts)
  4. Determined unresponsiveness: tapped shoulder; shouted 'Are you OK?' — Judge: 'Patient not responsive' (2 pts)
  5. Shouted to partner to activate ERS and get AED — Judge: 'AED not available' (2 pts)
  6. Checked breathing + pulse ≤10 sec (no less than 5): scanned chest for rise and fall (2 pts)
  7. OR: opened airway (head-tilt/chin-lift or modified jaw-thrust if spine suspected); simultaneously checked breathing and pulse ≤10 sec (2 pts)
  8. Carotid pulse check: 2–3 fingers in groove beside trachea — Judge: 'Not breathing, no pulse' (2 pts)
  9. Initial compressions: positioned self at patient's side (2 pts)
  10. Removed/moved bulky clothing from patient's chest (2 pts)
  11. Patient face up on firm flat surface (2 pts)
  12. Heel of one hand on center of chest, lower half of breastbone (2 pts)
  13. Heel of other hand on top of first (2 pts)
  14. Arms straight; shoulders directly over hands (2 pts)
  15. Rate 100–120/min; 30 compressions in 15–18 seconds (2 pts)
  16. Depth at least 2 inches (5 cm) (2 pts)
  17. Counted compressions aloud (2 pts)
  18. At end of each compression: allowed chest to fully recoil (2 pts)
  19. Placed mouth-to-mask device on face using bridge of nose as guide for correct position (2 pts)
  20. Sealed mask: index finger + thumb along top edge (hand closest to top of patient's head) (2 pts)
  21. Thumb of other hand along bottom edge of mask (2 pts)
  22. Remaining fingers along bony jaw margin; lifted jaw, performing head-tilt/chin-lift (2 pts)
  23. Pressed firmly around entire outside edge of mask to seal pocket mask against face (2 pts)
  24. Delivered 2 breaths over 1 second each to make chest rise (2 pts)
  25. Continued 30:2 cycles with minimal interruptions until Rescuer 2 arrives; when Rescuer 2 arrives, instructed them to take chest compressions (2 pts)
  26. Positioned self above patient's head for bag-mask when Rescuer 2 takes compressions (2 pts)
  27. E-C clamp technique — performed head tilt (2 pts)
  28. Placed mask using bridge of nose as guide (2 pts)
  29. Thumb + index finger made 'C' on side of mask pressing edges to face (2 pts)
  30. Remaining 3 fingers ('E') lifted angles of jaw; opened airway; pressed face to mask (2 pts)
  31. Squeezed bag to give each breath for 1 second after every 30 compressions; watched for chest rise (2 pts)
  32. Responded to Rescuer 2's call to switch after every 5 cycles; switch completed in <10 seconds; resumed compressions (2 pts)
  33. Continued two-person CPR; counting aloud; 30:2 ratio — Judge: 'EMS has arrived' (2 pts)
  34. Appropriate verbal and nonverbal communication with patient and personnel (2 pts)
  35. Gave patient information report to EMS rescuers (judges) (4 pts)
  36. Removed gloves without touching outside with bare hands (2 pts)
  37. Placed disposable PPE in biohazard receptacle (2 pts)
  38. Alcohol-based handrub (2 pts)

📌
2025–2026 Topic: Resolved: Artificial intelligence should replace physician judgment in primary clinical diagnosis. (Announced annually — confirm at hosacanada.ca)

📋 Overview

Two-member teams research and debate a biomedical resolution from both sides. Teams must demonstrate mastery of the scientific evidence and argue persuasively — the debate round tests whether you can dismantle your opponent's case while defending your own. Round 1 is a written knowledge exam; Round 2 is a live structured debate where your side (affirmative/negative) is assigned on the day.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Exam
50-item multiple-choice test covering the debate topic, biomedical science, and HOSA knowledge. 45 minutes. Both team members write independently; scores are averaged.
Round 2
Structured Debate
Teams are assigned Affirmative or Negative. Each team has 20 minutes of prep time, then presents constructive speeches (3 min each), rebuttals (2 min each), and closing statements (1 min each). Judges score on argument quality, evidence, rebuttal, and delivery.

🏆 What Judges Look For

📚
Scientific Knowledge — accuracy of facts, use of peer-reviewed evidence, correct biomedical terminology
🧠
Argumentation — logical structure, claim → evidence → warrant format, anticipating counterarguments
⚔️
Rebuttal Quality — directly addressing opponent's points, not just restating your case
🎤
Delivery — clarity, pace, eye contact, confidence, professional presence
🤝
Teamwork — coordinated transitions, building on partner's arguments, consistent messaging
⏱️
Time Management — staying within time limits; going over or under costs points

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Prepare both sides equally. You won't know your assigned position until the day — if you only researched one side, you're finished. Build a full argument map for affirmative and negative.
  2. Learn the evidence, not just the talking points. Judges on Round 2 are often scientists or clinicians. Citing actual studies (author, year, finding) wins points; vague references to "research shows" do not.
  3. Practice timed speeches out loud. Three minutes sounds long until you're standing in front of judges. Record yourself and cut anything that isn't advancing an argument.
  4. Drill your rebuttal. Most teams prepare constructive speeches but neglect rebuttal. Write out every major argument the other side could make and your exact counter for each.
  5. Coordinate with your partner. Decide who covers which arguments, practice your transitions, and agree on one consistent framing of your position so judges don't hear conflicting messages.
  6. For Round 1, study the topic from both sides. MCQs often test nuance — knowing why the opposing view has merit makes you a stronger writer and debater.

📋 Overview

Teams identify a health problem in their community, design an evidence-based awareness campaign, and present it to a panel of judges. This event rewards real-world health promotion skills — the best teams don't just describe a problem, they show a concrete plan that could actually be implemented in a school, neighbourhood, or online community. A written project report is submitted in advance.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Project Report (Pre-submitted)
Written document outlining: community health needs assessment, campaign goals and target audience, evidence-based strategies, implementation plan, evaluation methods, and budget (if applicable). Submitted before competition day.
Round 2
Oral Presentation + Q&A
10-minute live presentation of your project to judges, followed by 5 minutes of Q&A. Bring visual aids (poster, slides, or prototype materials). Judges will ask probing questions about your evidence base and implementation feasibility.

🏆 What Judges Look For

🔍
Needs Assessment — did you clearly identify a real community need with supporting data?
📖
Evidence Base — are your strategies backed by peer-reviewed public health research?
🎯
Goals & Measurability — SMART objectives (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound)
🗺️
Implementation Plan — realistic timeline, resources identified, roles assigned, barriers considered
📊
Evaluation — how will you know if it worked? pre/post surveys, metrics, follow-up plan
🎤
Presentation Quality — clarity, visual aids, team coordination, confident Q&A responses

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Choose a real, local issue. Projects grounded in your actual school or community are more convincing than generic campaigns. Local data (your school's survey results, a local health report) impresses judges far more than national statistics alone.
  2. Structure your report like a public health proposal. Follow the format: Problem Statement → Needs Assessment → Goals → Strategies → Implementation → Evaluation. Judges are often public health professionals — they recognise the standard framework.
  3. Cite Health Canada, CDC, and WHO sources. Your evidence base must come from credible health authorities. Avoid non-peer-reviewed sources.
  4. Build an evaluation plan before judges ask. The most common Q&A question is "how would you measure success?" Have a specific answer: "We'd administer a pre/post knowledge survey to 50 students and compare results at 6 weeks."
  5. Practice Q&A, not just the presentation. Assign one person on your team as the "devil's advocate" who asks hard questions during every practice session.
  6. Create a one-page visual summary. A well-designed infographic or flyer showing your campaign concept leaves a lasting impression and demonstrates you thought about real-world communication materials.

📋 Overview

Teams design and develop an original medical device, healthcare technology, diagnostic tool, or health solution. You present a working prototype or detailed model, explain the clinical problem it solves, the science behind it, and the path to real-world implementation. This is the most engineering-focused event at HOSA — creativity, scientific grounding, and practical feasibility are all scored.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Documentation (Pre-submitted)
A project report covering: the clinical problem and target patient population, existing solutions and their limitations, your innovation's design rationale, materials/technology used, proof-of-concept testing (if done), safety considerations, regulatory pathway (Health Canada/FDA), and estimated cost/feasibility.
Round 2
Prototype Presentation + Q&A
10-minute presentation with your physical prototype or detailed model. Demonstrate functionality where possible. Judges (often clinicians, engineers, or researchers) will ask detailed technical and clinical questions for 5 minutes.

🏆 What Judges Look For

💡
Originality — is this genuinely novel? a modification of existing tech must clearly improve on it
🩺
Clinical Relevance — does it solve a real, significant health problem? is the target population clearly defined?
⚗️
Scientific Validity — is the underlying science/engineering sound? are materials and mechanisms appropriate?
🏗️
Prototype Quality — does the prototype demonstrate the concept? functionality, not just aesthetics
📈
Feasibility & Scalability — could this realistically be manufactured, approved, and used? at what cost?
🛡️
Safety & Ethics — patient safety considerations, regulatory pathway, data privacy (if tech-based)

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Solve a specific problem, not a vague one. "Improving patient communication" is too broad. "A wristband that alerts nurses when a post-surgical patient's oxygen saturation drops below 92% without requiring bedside monitoring" is specific, clinical, and impressive.
  2. Research what already exists and explain why yours is better. Judges will immediately ask "doesn't X already do this?" Have a clear answer about how your innovation differs or improves on the current standard of care.
  3. Know the regulatory pathway. Every medical device in Canada needs Health Canada approval (Class I–IV). Knowing which class your device falls into and what the approval process involves signals serious preparation.
  4. Make your prototype do something. Even a simple demonstration — lights turning on, a sensor reading, a mechanism moving — is far more convincing than a static model. Use low-cost components (Arduino, 3D printing) if needed.
  5. Prepare for "what could go wrong?" Judges respect teams that have thought about failure modes, adverse events, and limitations. A team that says "our device has no risks" loses credibility instantly.
  6. Bring visuals beyond the prototype. A diagram of the mechanism, a photo of testing, a comparison table vs. existing solutions, or a patient use-case scenario all help judges understand your thinking.

📋 Overview

An individual event combining a written knowledge exam with a hands-on clinical skills demonstration in a specific healthcare specialty. Competitors choose (or are assigned) from areas such as Medical Assisting, Nursing Assisting, Home Health Aide, or Phlebotomy. Round 1 tests specialty-specific knowledge; Round 2 evaluates clinical technique at a simulated patient station. Strong performance requires both theoretical knowledge and practiced hands-on competency.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Examination
50 multiple-choice questions covering specialty-specific clinical knowledge: anatomy, patient care procedures, infection control, professional standards, and safety protocols. 45 minutes. Varies by chosen specialty.
Round 2
Clinical Skills Demonstration
Perform 2–4 assigned clinical skills at a simulated patient station. Tasks are drawn from the specialty skill list (e.g., vital signs, wound care, catheter care, venipuncture). Evaluated by a licensed clinician using a standardised checklist. Typically 15–20 minutes.

🏆 What Judges Look For

📚
Theoretical Knowledge — accuracy on written exam; understanding of why procedures are performed, not just how
🙌
Technical Technique — correct, safe performance of each skill step per standardised protocol
🛡️
Infection Control — hand hygiene, PPE use, aseptic technique — judges deduct heavily for lapses
🗣️
Patient Communication — introducing yourself, explaining procedures, obtaining consent, professional bedside manner
⚠️
Safety Awareness — identifying and managing risks (fall prevention, sharps disposal, patient positioning)
⏱️
Efficiency & Confidence — completing skills within the time frame without hesitation or excessive correction

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Practise skills until they're automatic. Under competition stress, you will default to what you've drilled. Practise each skill so many times you can do it correctly while being watched and talking aloud.
  2. Narrate your actions during skills. Say what you're doing as you do it ("I am now washing my hands for 20 seconds…"). This communicates knowledge to the judge even if your technique has a minor imperfection.
  3. Never skip infection control steps. Judges are often registered nurses or clinicians. They fail competitors who skip hand hygiene, miss PPE, or break sterile field, regardless of how well everything else went.
  4. Study the skill checklist, not just the textbook. HOSA publishes the exact skill steps used to evaluate you. Practise to that checklist precisely — the order of steps matters.
  5. Simulate realistic conditions. Practise on a manikin or with a partner (not just talking through steps). Practice in scrubs, with equipment, at a table set up like a skills station.
  6. For the written exam, focus on 'why' not just 'what'. MCQs often test clinical reasoning ("why would you NOT do X in this situation") rather than recall. Understanding the rationale behind each procedure prepares you for scenario-based questions.

📋 Overview

Teams develop and present a mental health awareness and promotion project targeting a specific population (most commonly youth, schools, or underserved communities). The event evaluates your ability to apply evidence-based mental health promotion strategies, reduce stigma, and create materials that could realistically be used in a real community setting. Strong projects go beyond "awareness" to offer concrete action steps and measurable outcomes.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Project (Pre-submitted)
Documentation includes: population needs assessment with supporting data, project goals aligned to mental health promotion frameworks (e.g., Ottawa Charter), evidence-based strategies, anti-stigma approach, implementation timeline, partnership plan (schools, community orgs), and evaluation methodology.
Round 2
Oral Presentation + Q&A
10-minute presentation to judges, followed by Q&A. Bring materials: posters, brochures, social media mockups, or a demo of any digital tools. Judges often include mental health professionals who will probe your clinical accuracy and sensitivity of language.

🏆 What Judges Look For

📊
Needs Assessment — evidence that the target population actually has the identified mental health need
🔬
Evidence-Based Approach — strategies grounded in peer-reviewed mental health promotion literature, not just opinion
❤️
Anti-Stigma Framing — language is person-first, non-stigmatising, and avoids harmful stereotypes
🎯
Clear Objectives — specific, measurable goals tied to mental health outcomes, not just engagement metrics
🛠️
Implementation Feasibility — realistic plan given school or community resources; clear partnership strategy
📏
Evaluation Plan — how will you measure attitude change, stigma reduction, or help-seeking behaviour?

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Use person-first, recovery-oriented language throughout. Judges who are mental health professionals will immediately notice stigmatising language ("mentally ill person" vs. "person living with a mental health condition"). Review the Mental Health Commission of Canada's language guide.
  2. Cite Canadian-specific data. Statistics Canada, the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (CAMH), and the Mental Health Commission of Canada publish Canadian youth mental health statistics that are more relevant than U.S. data.
  3. Go beyond awareness — address help-seeking barriers. "Raising awareness" alone scores average marks. Projects that specifically target barriers (stigma, cost, access, cultural), and explain how to reduce them, score in the top tier.
  4. Design for a specific population, not "everyone". A project for Indigenous youth in remote communities is stronger than a project for "young people" because it demonstrates a real needs assessment and culturally appropriate design.
  5. Know the Ottawa Charter's five action areas. Many judges frame mental health promotion through this international framework (building healthy public policy, creating supportive environments, strengthening community action, developing personal skills, reorienting health services). Structuring your project around these earns recognition.
  6. Include actual materials, not just descriptions. Bring a sample brochure, social media post, or workshop outline. Judges can see immediately whether your campaign would work in the real world.

📋 Overview

Teams apply public health principles to research a current health topic, develop a public health intervention or policy recommendation, and present it to judges. Round 1 is a written exam on core public health concepts; Round 2 is a project presentation. 🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Examination
50 MCQs on public health concepts: epidemiology fundamentals, biostatistics, health determinants, Canadian public health infrastructure (PHAC, provincial agencies), surveillance systems, disease prevention levels, and the current competition topic. 45 minutes.
Round 2
Project Presentation + Q&A
10-minute presentation of a public health intervention or policy recommendation addressing the year's theme. Backed by data, grounded in public health frameworks, and realistic for implementation at the community or national level. 5-minute Q&A from a panel that may include public health professionals.

🏆 What Judges Look For

📐
Public Health Framework — correct application of epidemiological concepts, Haddon Matrix, or Ottawa Charter
📊
Use of Evidence — data from credible public health sources (PHAC, Health Canada, WHO, CDC)
🎯
Intervention Design — specific, feasible, and appropriate for the target population and setting
⚖️
Policy Awareness — understanding of regulatory context, stakeholders, and implementation barriers
📏
Evaluation Methodology — how the intervention's effectiveness would be measured; appropriate metrics
🗺️
Canadian Context — awareness of Canadian health system, provincial variation, and relevant legislation

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Know the three levels of disease prevention cold. Primary (prevent before onset), secondary (early detection), tertiary (limit disability) — MCQs frequently test application of these, not just definitions.

  3. Study the social determinants of health using a Canadian lens. Income, education, housing, and Indigenous health disparities are central to Canadian public health discourse. Know the Marmot Commission and Health Canada's SDOH framework.
  4. For your project, propose something at the right scale. Recommending that "Canada ban processed food" is not implementable. A school-based antimicrobial stewardship programme for a specific school board is concrete, achievable, and demonstrably evidence-based.
  5. Build in a surveillance component. Strong public health projects include a data monitoring plan — what data will be collected, by whom, how often, and how it feeds back into programme adjustment.
  6. Know the competition topic in depth. The MCQ exam will have at least 15–20 questions directly related to the year's theme. Read three to five key papers on it (PubMed, CMAJ), know the Canadian burden of the problem, and understand the major policy debates.

📋 Overview

An individual speaking event that tests both research ability and persuasive communication. On the day, competitors receive a health-related topic and have 30 minutes to prepare a 5-minute persuasive speech using provided reference materials. No memorisation is needed — the topic is revealed at competition, and you are assessed on how convincingly you argue a position with the resources given. This event rewards quick critical reading, logical argument construction, and confident delivery under pressure.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Prep
30-Minute Research Phase
You receive a packet of reference materials (articles, statistics, excerpts) on the topic. You may write notes. No internet access. Your job: identify the strongest evidence, build a persuasive argument structure, and prepare for likely Q&A challenges.
Round 1
Persuasive Speech (5 minutes)
Deliver your prepared speech to a panel of judges. Begin with a compelling hook, state your position clearly, present 2–3 evidence-backed arguments, address the strongest counterargument, and close with a call to action. You may use brief notes but not a full script.
Q&A
Judge Questions (3 minutes)
Judges probe your reasoning, challenge your evidence, and test your ability to defend your position under pressure. This segment can significantly change your score — confident, substantive responses distinguish top competitors.

🏆 What Judges Look For

🔍
Research Use — accurately and effectively drawing on the provided materials; not misrepresenting sources
🧩
Argument Structure — clear thesis, logical progression, addressing the strongest counterargument
📖
Evidence Quality — choosing the most relevant and compelling evidence from the packet; statistics cited correctly
🎯
Persuasiveness — does the speech actually change minds? compelling framing, emotional resonance balanced with logic
🗣️
Delivery — pace, eye contact, vocal variety, confidence, minimal filler words
💪
Q&A Defence — direct answers, acknowledging nuance without abandoning your position, staying composed

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Spend the first 5 minutes of prep skimming for the strongest data point. One powerful statistic or study will anchor your entire speech. Find it first, then build your argument around it.
  2. Structure every speech the same way before competition day. Hook → Position → Argument 1 → Argument 2 → Concede + Rebut → Call to Action. Practise this structure on different health topics so it's automatic by competition day.
  3. Prepare for the counterargument — pick it apart specifically. Judges reward speakers who say "Opponents argue X — but this misses Y because of Z" rather than just ignoring the other side. Acknowledge the steelman version of the counterargument, then dismantle it.
  4. Know common Canadian health policy contexts cold. Tobacco regulation, cannabis policy, universal pharmacare, vaccine mandates, and mental health funding debates are perennial persuasive speaking topics. Having background knowledge before you see the packet saves critical prep time.
  5. For Q&A, listen carefully and answer the actual question. The most common mistake is deflecting to a pre-prepared talking point. Judges notice. Answer directly first, then expand.
  6. Practise on a 5-minute timer weekly. Five minutes is both shorter and longer than it feels. Record yourself. Cut anything that doesn't serve the argument. Practise until your pacing feels comfortable without a script.

📋 Overview

Teams conduct original health science research — a proper study with a hypothesis, methodology, data collection, analysis, and conclusion — and present findings on a standardised research poster. This is the most academically rigorous HOSA event. Round 1 is a written exam on research methodology; Round 2 is a poster session where judges walk up to your display and ask you to present and defend your research. Teams that have conducted real original research (even small-scale surveys or lab work) have a significant advantage.

🗓 Format & Rounds

Round 1
Written Examination
50 MCQs on research methodology: study design types, hypothesis development, sampling, data collection, statistical analysis basics (mean, standard deviation, p-value, confidence intervals), research ethics (REB/IRB), APA citation, and interpreting results. 45 minutes.
Round 2
Poster Session + Q&A
Display your research poster at an assigned station. Judges rotate through; you deliver a 5–8 minute summary of your research and answer 5–10 minutes of detailed questions. Judges may include university researchers or clinicians with methodological expertise.

🏆 What Judges Look For

🔬
Research Question & Hypothesis — PICO-formatted question, testable hypothesis, clearly defined variables
🗂️
Methodology — appropriate study design for the question; sampling strategy; data collection validity
📈
Analysis & Results — correct statistical approach; accurate presentation of findings; appropriate tables/graphs
💬
Discussion & Conclusion — interpreting results in context of existing literature; acknowledging limitations
🖼️
Poster Design — clear visual hierarchy, readable fonts, logical flow, appropriate use of figures
🎓
Research Defence — explaining methodological choices, defending conclusions, acknowledging limitations honestly

💡 Prep Tips

  1. Conduct real original research — even a simple survey counts. A team that surveyed 80 students at their school about sleep habits and correlated it with academic performance is doing original research. It doesn't need to be a lab study. IRB/REB ethics approval strengthens your project significantly.
  2. Use PICO format to write your research question. Population, Intervention/Exposure, Comparison, Outcome. "In (P) high school students, does (I) daily moderate exercise, compared to (C) sedentary behaviour, reduce (O) self-reported anxiety scores?" is a proper research question.
  3. Know the difference between correlation and causation — and say so in your discussion. Judges will test this. If your study design is observational, you cannot claim causation, and trying to will immediately undermine your credibility.
  4. Study your poster as much as your project. The poster should tell the complete story without you speaking: Introduction → Methods → Results → Discussion → Conclusion. Each section should be scannable in 30 seconds.
  5. Prepare for "what are the limitations of your study?" Every research project has limitations (small sample, convenience sampling, self-report bias, lack of control group). Know yours and say them confidently before judges point them out.
  6. For the written exam, focus on study design and statistics. MCQs heavily test: RCT vs. cohort vs. case-control, Type I vs. Type II error, what a p-value means, how to interpret a confidence interval, and research ethics principles (Belmont Report, autonomy, beneficence, justice).
